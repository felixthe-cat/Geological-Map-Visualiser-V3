How It's Done

GeoVisualise turns Hong Kong's public ground-investigation records into borehole logs, cross-sections, rockhead contours and a 3D block of the ground. This page explains where the data comes from, how the results are worked out, what you can do with them, and where they stop being reliable.

Part 1How the data is collected

Nothing is surveyed by this website. Every input is an open dataset published by the Hong Kong Government, fetched when you ask for it.

Boreholes. Contractors drill boreholes for public works and private developments and log the soil and rock. The Geotechnical Engineering Office (GEO) of CEDD keeps these records and publishes them. The map shows about 330,000 borehole locations; around 190,000 of them come with a full digital log in AGS format.
Ground levels. Two terrain models are used. The Lands Department's 5 m model covers all of Hong Kong. CEDD's 2020 airborne LiDAR survey (a plane scanning the ground with a laser) gives a 0.5 m bare-earth model, which this site holds for the ground within 200 m of AGS boreholes.
Buildings. The Lands Department's building map gives each building's outline with its base and roof level.
Roads and bridges. The Lands Department publishes road centre lines. The Highways Department publishes the outlines of the structures it maintains: bridges, flyovers and footbridges.
Photo map. The optional photo-realistic 3D map is the Lands Department's 3D mesh, built from overlapping aerial photographs.

When you pick a site

  1. You draw a rectangle on the site map. The site lists the boreholes inside it.
  2. For boreholes with AGS data, the site reads just those reports out of the CEDD archive, not the whole archive.
  3. Each log is read into layers: top depth, base depth, material, and weathering grade.
  4. Ground levels, buildings, roads and bridges for the same area are fetched when you open the section or the 3D tab.

Part 2The engineering methods used

The site follows normal Hong Kong practice: Geoguide 3 for naming materials, straight-line joining of layers between boreholes, and Grade III as the top of rock.

Reading and classifying a borehole log

  • Three tables are read from each AGS file: location (LOCA or HOLE), layers (GEOL) and weathering (WETH).
  • Each layer gets a material name (fill, alluvium, colluvium, marine deposit, weathered rock) and, for rock, a decomposition grade I to VI as defined in Geoguide 3. Grade VI is residual soil; Grade I is fresh rock.
  • The grade comes from the most reliable field available: the weathering table first, then the origin code, then the written description.

2D cross-section

  • You draw a section line A to B. Boreholes within a distance tolerance (100 m unless you change it) are moved square onto the line.
  • The same layer in neighbouring boreholes is joined. You choose how: straight lines (the classic hand-drawn way), a smooth curve that cannot overshoot, or stepped with no joining at all.
  • The ground line follows the 5 m terrain model, shifted to agree with each borehole's surveyed ground level at the borehole's own position.
  • Each borehole's distance from the line is printed, because a borehole far from the line says less about the ground under it.

Rockhead contours

  • Rockhead is the top of the first layer at Grade III or better (Grade II if you choose). This is the usual Hong Kong definition of "rock".
  • The rockhead levels are spread across the site by inverse-distance weighting: every point takes a weighted average of the boreholes, with nearer boreholes counting more.
  • Boreholes that never reached rock are marked "rock not encountered". They are not guessed.

3D ground block

  • All layers are put in one order, shallowest first, using their average depth across the boreholes.
  • The top of the block is the real ground (LiDAR or the 5 m model), not a surface drawn between borehole tops.
  • For each layer, its thickness is spread across the site by inverse-distance weighting (power 2), then hung below the layer above.
  • A layer missing from a borehole has zero thickness there, so it thins out and disappears between boreholes. It can never turn upside down.
  • The deepest layer runs down to the block's base, 5 m below the deepest borehole unless you set a level.
  • Cutting the block samples the same model along the cut line, so the 3D section and the block always agree.

Buildings, roads and bridges in the 3D view

  • Buildings are pushed up from their outline to their roof level as plain white blocks.
  • Bridges and flyovers use the Highways Department outline. The outline has no height, so the deck level is read from the 5 m terrain model, which includes elevated roads (the LiDAR model does not).
  • Roads on the ground use the Lands Department centre line, widened to a typical width for the road type: 14 m highway, 11 m main road, 7.3 m secondary road, 5 m restricted road, 3 m track.
  • Everything is trimmed to your site boundary and is cut away with the ground when you slice a section.

All positions use the Hong Kong 1980 Grid (eastings and northings in metres). All levels are in mPD (metres above Principal Datum).

Typical uses in the design stages

  • Desk study. See what the ground probably is before spending money on site.
  • Planning new boreholes. The 3D block shows where boreholes are far apart or where a layer changes quickly. Drill there.
  • Preliminary design. Cut a section through a slope, an excavation or a foundation line and take it into SLOPE/W or PLAXIS as a starting geometry.
  • Checking a model by others. Compare a consultant's ground model with the public records.
  • Communication. A white-model 3D view is easier for non-engineers to read than a borehole log.
The export gives geometry only. Strength, stiffness, permeability and groundwater are not in the public borehole logs in a usable form. You must choose design parameters and water levels yourself, from testing and from your own judgement.

Part 6Assumptions and limitations

The model is a concept model. It is estimated between boreholes and is not for construction or final design.

Assumptions built into the model

  • Layers come in one order everywhere. A material that appears twice in a borehole (for example fill, then colluvium, then fill again) cannot be shown as two separate bodies.
  • Layer thickness changes smoothly between boreholes. The method knows distance only. It knows nothing about faults, buried stream channels, dykes, or how the rock was formed.
  • Boreholes are vertical and their recorded position and ground level are correct.
  • The ground has not changed since it was surveyed. A borehole drilled before a site was cut or filled will not match today's ground.
  • Grade III is the top of rock. Corestones (hard boulders left in weathered rock) can be logged as rock and give a false, shallow rockhead.

Limits of the data

ItemLimit
Borehole logsLogged by many contractors over decades. Naming and quality vary. Some records are missing or wrongly located.
5 m terrain modelStated accuracy about ±5 m. It includes elevated roads and bridges, so it is not always the ground.
0.5 m LiDAR modelSurveyed in 2020; later earthworks are not shown. Held only within 200 m of AGS boreholes; the 5 m model is used elsewhere.
BuildingsFlat-topped blocks from outline and roof level. No roof shapes, podium steps, basements or foundations.
Roads on the groundReal centre line, assumed width by road type. Not the real kerb lines. Tunnels are not drawn.
Bridges and flyoversReal outline, estimated deck level (good to a few metres), assumed 1.5 m thick. No piers, abutments or ramps' real gradients.
Section projectionA borehole 80 m off the line is drawn as if it were on the line. Check the printed offsets.
Beyond the boreholesOutside the outermost boreholes the layers are extended, not measured. Treat the edges of a block with extra caution.

What the model does not include

  • Groundwater levels and pore pressures.
  • Soil and rock strength or stiffness parameters.
  • Rock joints, faults and other structure.
  • Underground utilities, tunnels, basements and existing foundations.
Always confirm with site-specific ground investigation and the judgement of a qualified geotechnical engineer before any design decision. This website is a visualisation aid, not a substitute for either.

Part 7Further reading

Official sources on how Hong Kong's ground, terrain and map data are produced.

How ground investigation and logging are done

  • Geoguide 2: Guide to Site Investigation (GEO). How boreholes are planned, drilled, sampled and reported.
  • Geoguide 3: Guide to Rock and Soil Descriptions (GEO). The decomposition grades I to VI and the naming used on this site.
  • All Geoguides, including Geoguide 1 (retaining walls) and Geoguide 7 (soil nailing).
  • AGS data format (Association of Geotechnical and Geoenvironmental Specialists). The file format the borehole records are published in.

How Hong Kong's geological maps are made

  • Hong Kong Geological Survey (GEO). The published geological maps, sheet reports and memoirs.
  • The Geology of Hong Kong (interactive on-line) (GEO). The rocks, superficial deposits and geological history of the territory.
  • GEO Reports. Technical reports on weathering, landslides, rockhead and ground models.
  • GInfo. GEO's map viewer for boreholes, geology and slope records.

How the terrain data is made

  • LiDAR data, GEO open data. The 2010 and 2020 territory-wide airborne LiDAR surveys and how to obtain them.
  • Digital Terrain Model, 5 m grid (Lands Department). What it contains and its stated accuracy.
  • Hong Kong Geodetic Survey Services (Lands Department). The Hong Kong 1980 Grid and the Principal Datum.

How the building, road and 3D map data is made

  • Lands Department open geospatial data. The digital topographic maps, the 3D digital map and the 3D spatial data, with notes on each.
  • Digital Topographic Map iB1000 (Lands Department). The 1:1,000 base map that buildings and roads are drawn from.
  • 3D map viewer (Lands Department). The photo-realistic 3D model of Hong Kong.
  • CSDI portal. The government's shared map-data portal, where the building, road and structure layers are published.

The analysis software mentioned above

  • GeoStudio SLOPE/W (Seequent). Slope stability analysis.
  • PLAXIS 2D (Bentley). Finite-element analysis of soil and rock.
